1. THE DIGESTIVE SYSTEM

   Digestion: food particles are broken down to smaller components which are later
   absorbed by the body. This happens by both physical means such as chewing and by
   chemical means such as enzymatic processes.
   Digestive Tract: Organs that food and liquids travel through when they are swallowed,
   digested, absorbed, and leave the body as feces. It includes the mouth, pharynx (throat),
   esophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, rectum, and anus.

2. THE DIGESTIVE TRACT

   The digestive system is made up of the gastrointestinal tract, the liver, pancreas, and
   gallbladder.
   The 3 is a series of organs that form a tube from the mouth to the anus.
   The hollow organs that make up the GI tract are the mouth, esophagus, stomach, small
   intestine, large intestine, and anus.
   The liver, pancreas, and gallbladder are the solid organs of the digestive system.
   Steps:
   The tongue (skeletal muscle), and the smooth muscles of the pharynx and
   esophagus swallow the bolus of food, which enters the esophagus.
   Within the esophagus, muscular contraction, known as peristalsis , pushes the bolus
   and liquid from the mouth into the stomach.
   Once the bolus has gotten to the top of the stomach, it must first pass through the
   lower esophageal sphincter. This is a muscle that controls food entering the
   stomach and exiting the stomach back into the esophagus.
   The lower esophageal sphincter relaxes, letting the bolus into the upper portion of the
   stomach, and will contract again to prevent food from flowing back into the
   esophagus.
   Through this process, the food begins to mix through peristalsis from the fundus, the
   upper portion of the stomach to the corpus, the midportion, and then to the antrum
   and pylorus, the lower portion of the stomach.
   The food is now mixed into chyme. This mixture of chyme continuously mixes and is
   slowly released in small quantities into the small intestine through the lower pyloric
   sphincter. The lower pyloric sphincter is a muscle that relaxes to slowly release small
   amounts of the contents of the stomach and then contracts to help prevent backflow
   into the stomach.
   The contents next enter the small intestine, which is broken down into three parts, the
   duodenum, the jejunum, and the ileum. When the chyme enters the duodenum, the
   pancreas, gallbladder, and duodenal epithelium secrete more enzymes that contain
   sodium bicarbonate. Most of the digestion occurs within the duodenum and upper
   part of the jejunum.
   Bile is released into the small intestine from the gallbladder. Bile contains bile salts
   that promote digestion and absorption of fats, cholesterol, and fat-soluble vitamins. A
   large number of enzymes are released that play a large part in hydrolyzing different
   parts of the makeup of food. The enzymes are pancreatic lipase and amylase, trypsin,
   chymotrypsin, carboxypeptidase, aminopeptidase, ribonuclease, and
   deoxyribonuclease. These enzymes do a combination of hydrolysis, forming
   monoglycerides, fatty acids, esters, dextrins, maltose, peptide bonds, amino acids,
   and mononucleotides. At this point, the ileocecal valve is a muscle that is helping
   control the amount of chyme that is leaving the small intestine and goes into the large
   intestine.

   The chyme, which has been down into smaller particles from the enzymes in the small
   intestine, now entered the large intestine. The mixture still contains complex starches
   and ingested fiber.
   The large intestine is a 5- to 6-foot-long muscular tube that connects the cecum,
   ascending colon, transverse colon, descending colon, and sigmoid colon as it
   connects to the rectum.
   The chyme enters the large intestine, and absorption, as well as bacterial
   fermentation, begins. Most of the fluid that was consumed is absorbed through the
   large intestine, and the rest is excreted out.
   The colon ferments the rest of the carbohydrates and amino acids and synthesizes
   some vitamins. Whatever contents are not reabsorbed back into the body, are later
   excreted out through the anal tract.

3. THE FOOD DIGESTION PROCESS

   What role does the esophagus play in food digestion? After you swallow, peristalsis
   pushes the food down your esophagus into your stomach.
   Where in your body is bile produced? Your liver makes a digestive fluid called bile that
   helps digest fats and some vitamins. Bile ducts carry bile from your liver to your
   gallbladder for storage, or to the small intestine for use.
   What is the function of the gallbladder? Your gallbladder stores bile between meals.
   When you eat, your gallbladder squeezes bile through the bile ducts into your small
   intestine.

   Where in your body does the primary breakdown of proteins, carbohydrates, and fats
   occur? Your small intestine makes digestive juice, which mixes with bile and pancreatic
   juice to complete the breakdown of proteins, carbohydrates, and fats. Bacteria in your
   small intestine make some of the enzymes you need to digest carbohydrates. Your small
   intestine moves water from your bloodstream into your GI tract to help break down food.
   Your small intestine also absorbs water with other nutrients.
   Where in your body is stool (or feces) formed? In your large intestine, more water moves
   from your GI tract into your bloodstream. Bacteria in your large intestine help break down
   remaining nutrients and make vitamin K. Waste products of digestion, including parts of
   food that are still too large, become stool.

1. INTRODUCTION TO ABSORPTION

   Absorption is a process in which the proper nutrients are extracted from the broken-down
   food and transported to the proper organ. The small intestine is the primary organ for
   absorption within the digestive system.
   The main organ for absorption is the small intestine, with some absorption also within the
   stomach and large intestine. Absorption is one of the most important aspects of nutrient
   digestion because if the food is not broken down and properly absorbed, the body
   cannot use it. The absorption process helps turn these nutrients into usable aspects for
   the cells throughout the body.
   While in the stomach, the food is broken down by enzymes, hydrochloric acid, and the
   process of churning. Minimal absorption occurs within the stomach, mostly just water is
   absorbed here.

2. ABSORPTION IN THE SMALL INTESTINE

   The small intestine has a 10-foot length, which provides a surface area equivalent to 1.5
   bowling lanes.
   To remove the absorbed molecules rapidly and provide room for more to be absorbed, a
   rush of circulating blood continuously washes the underside of this surface, carrying the
   absorbed nutrients away to the liver and other parts of the body. Absorption of nutrients
   into intestinal cells typically occurs by simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion, or active
   transport.
   The small intestine is separated into three sections, the duodenum, the jejunum, and the
   ileum. The inner surface of the small intestine looks smooth but is actually wrinkled into
   hundreds of folds. Each fold has thousands of fingerlike projections, known as villi, each
   covered with microvilli. Within the spaces between villi are crypts. Crypts are glands that
   secrete intestinal juices into the small intestine whereas the goblet cells secrete mucus.
   Most of the absorption occurs within the jejunum portion of the small intestine except for
   iron, which is absorbed in the duodenum, and vitamin B12 and bile salts are absorbed in
   the ileum.
   The microvilli have epithelial cells, which play a large part in the absorption. The epithelial
   cells transport the nutrients digested in the small intestine into the capillaries and
   lacteals.
   The lacteals are lymphatic vessels that receive the lipids and fat-soluble vitamins from the
   epithelial cells. The eventually transport lipids (as triglycerides) into the bloodstream via
   the subclavian vein.
   The capillaries receive amino acids and carbohydrates. Once a nutrient has entered the
   bloodstream, it may be transported to any of the cells in the body.

3. ABSORBING NUTRIENTS

   Nutrients are absorbed and transported through either active transport or passive
   transport.
   Passive transport : are moving from an area of high concentration to a lower
   concentration. Passive transport does not always require any transport proteins or
   adenosine triphosphate (simple diffusion).
   - If the passive transport utilizes a transport protein this is passive diffusion , and if the
     transport protein assists the nutrients in crossing the brush border membrane, this is
     facilitated diffusion.
     Active transport : nutrients are transported to an area within the body that has a
     concentration differing or against the nutrient's concentration. Due to the resistance of

transportation because of the varying concentrations, a transporter protein and
adenosine triphosphate (ATP) energy are required.

    Against their gradient means moving from low to high, opposite of passive.

4. BLOOD AND ABSORPTION

   All body tissues get nutrients and oxygen from the blood and deposit carbon dioxide and
   other wastes back into the blood. The digestive system provides nutrients and the lungs
   exchange oxygen and carbon dioxide. The kidneys filter wastes out of the blood to be
   excreted in the urine.
   - Right side of the heart ➡ lungs ➡ left side of the heart ➡ aorta (main artery) ➡

5. ABSORPTION IN THE LARGE INTESTINE

   After the food is finished absorption through the small intestine, it passes through the
   large intestine. While in the large intestine, the food is fermented and formed into waste
   to be later excreted. The only absorption that occurs within the large intestine is
   remaining water and electrolytes.

3. INTRODUCTION TO ADENOSINE TRIPHOSPHATE

   The breakdown of glucose, fatty acids, protein, and amino acids releases energy. This
   energy can be captured in ATP (adenosine triphosphate).
   ATP is an energy-carrying molecule found in the cells of all living things. ATP captures

   carrier and can deliver energy to places within the cell where it is needed.

1.  ADENOSINE TRIPHOSPHATE AND DIGESTION

    Chemical reactions in metabolic pathways rarely take place spontaneously. Each reaction
    step is facilitated, or catalyzed, by a protein called an enzyme. Some enzymes can’t
    function without their coenzyme. Many of the coenzymes we need come from B
    vitamins, this is why we need to eat a variety of foods each day.
    Carbohydrates are broken down into glucose and other monosaccharides. Proteins are
    broken down into amino acids. Fats are broken down into fatty acids and glycerol.
    When our bodies use catabolism, the bonds between these atoms break and
    release energy. Glucose has 6 carbons, glycerol has 3 carbons, fatty acids may have 20
    or more carbons, and amino acids may have up to 5 carbons. Depending on how many
    carbons there are, is how much energy can be provided when the bonds are broken
    down.
    Inside the cell, each sugar molecule is broken down through a complex series of
    chemical reactions. As chemical energy is released from the bonds in the
    monosaccharide, it is harnessed to synthesize high-energy adenosine triphosphate (ATP)
    molecules. ATP is the primary energy currency of all cells, cells use molecules of ATP to
    perform immediate work and power chemical reactions.
    Glycolysis is the first of the main metabolic pathways of cellular respiration to produce
    energy in the form of ATP. Through two distinct phases, the six-carbon ring of glucose is
    cleaved into two three-carbon sugars of pyruvate through a series of enzymatic
    reactions. The first phase of glycolysis requires energy, while the second phase
    completes the conversion to pyruvate and produces ATP and NADH for the cell to use for
    energy. Overall, the process of glycolysis produces a net gain of two pyruvate molecules,
    two ATP molecules, and two NADH molecules for the cell to use for energy.
    Following the conversion of glucose to pyruvate, the glycolytic pathway is linked to the
    Krebs Cycle (citric acid cycle / TCA cycle), where further ATP will be produced for the
    cell’s energy needs.

    During cellular respiration, a glucose molecule is gradually broken down into carbon
    dioxide and water. Along the way, some ATP is produced directly in the reactions that
    transform glucose. Much more ATP, however, is produced later in a process called
    oxidative phosphorylation. Oxidative phosphorylation is powered by the movement of
    electrons through the electron transport chain, a series of proteins embedded in the inner
    membrane of the mitochondrion.
    The four stages of cellular respiration:

        Glycolysis. In glycolysis, glucose—a six-carbon sugar—undergoes a series of
        chemical transformations. In the end, it gets converted into two molecules of
        pyruvate, a three-carbon organic molecule.
        ‣ 1 Glucose => Net 2 Pyruvate, 2NADH, 2 ATP (produces 4 ATP, but 2 are needed for
        first step of glycolysis)

        Pyruvate oxidation. Each pyruvate from glycolysis goes into the mitochondrial matrix
        —the innermost compartment of mitochondria. There, it’s converted into a two-
        carbon molecule bound to Coenzyme A, known as acetyl CoA. Carbon dioxide is
        released and NADH is generated.
        ‣ 2 Pyruvate => 2 CoA, 2 NADH

        Citric acid cycle (Krebs cycle). Still in mitochondrial matrix. The acetyl CoA made in
        the last step combines with a four-carbon oxaloacetate molecule and goes through a
        cycle of reactions, ultimately regenerating the oxaloacetate starting molecule.
        ‣ 2 COA => 2 ATP, 6 NADH, 2 FADH 2 + CO 2

        Oxidative phosphorylation / electron transport chain. Still in mitochondria - at the
        inner membrane. The NADH and FADH 2 made in other steps deposit their electrons
        in the electron transport chain, turning back into their "empty" forms ( NAD+ and FAD).
        As electrons move down the chain, energy is released and used to pump protons out
        of the matrix, forming a gradient. Oxygen is needed to accept the electrons at the end
        of the chain, they bind to oxygen forming H 2 O. Protons flow back into the matrix
        through an enzyme called ATP synthase, making ATP. At the end of the electron

transport chain, oxygen accepts electrons and takes up protons to form water. This
step makes the most ATP (26-34)

    Glycolysis can take place without oxygen in a process called fermentation. The other
    three stages of cellular respiration—pyruvate oxidation, the citric acid cycle, and
    oxidative phosphorylation—require oxygen in order to occur.

2. METABOLISM WITHOUT OXYGEN

   Anaerobic respiration is a type of respiration where oxygen is not used; instead, organic
   or inorganic molecules are used as final electron acceptors.
   Processes that use an organic molecule to regenerate NAD+ from NADH are collectively
   referred to as fermentation.
   Some living systems use an inorganic molecule as a final electron acceptor. Both
   methods are called anaerobic cellular respiration, where organisms convert energy for
   their use in the absence of oxygen.
   The fermentation method used by animals and certain bacteria (like those in yogurt) is
   called lactic acid fermentation. This type of fermentation is used routinely in mammalian
   red blood cells and in skeletal muscle that has an insufficient oxygen supply to allow
   aerobic respiration to continue (that is, in muscles used to the point of fatigue). The
   excess amount of lactate in those muscles is what causes the burning sensation in your
   legs while running. In these muscles, lactic acid accumulation must be removed by the
   blood circulation and the lactate brought to the liver for further metabolism.
   Glycogen, a polymer of glucose, is an energy-storage molecule in animals. When there is
   adequate ATP present, excess glucose is shunted into glycogen for storage. Glycogen is
   made and stored in both the liver and muscles.
   - Glycogen is hydrolyzed into the glucose monomer, glucose-1-phosphate (G-1-P), if
     blood sugar levels drop.

2.  HEALTH OF THE GASTROINTESTINAL TRACT

3.  PROBLEMS WITH DIGESTION
    a.CELIAC DISEASE AND GLUTEN

        Celiac disease is an autoimmune disorder where the ingestion of gluten—a protein found
        in wheat, barley, and rye—triggers an immune response that damages the small intestine,
        leading to malabsorption, nutrient deficiencies, and a host of other health issues.
        Many people with celiac disease have symptoms outside of the digestive system, making
        it harder to diagnose. In fact, some individuals may experience no gastrointestinal
        symptoms at all, which is why celiac disease can be misdiagnosed as other conditions
        like irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) or even psychiatric disorders.
        The only effective treatment for celiac disease is a strict, lifelong gluten-free diet.

b.GASTROESOPHAGEAL REFLUX DISEASE (GERD)

    Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is a chronic digestive condition where stomach
    acid or, occasionally, bile irritates the lining of the esophagus. This occurs when the lower
    esophageal sphincter (LES), a muscle that acts as a valve between the esophagus and
    the stomach, relaxes or weakens inappropriately, allowing stomach contents to flow back
    into the esophagus. GERD is more severe and persistent than occasional acid reflux, and
    it can lead to complications if left untreated.
    If left untreated, GERD can lead to serious complications:
        Esophagitis: Inflammation of the esophagus, often resulting from chronic acid
        exposure, can lead to ulcers, bleeding, and pain.
        Strictures: Chronic inflammation can lead to the formation of scar tissue, which
        narrows the esophagus and makes swallowing difficult.
        Barrett's esophagus: This is a condition where the cells lining the esophagus change
        due to chronic acid exposure. It increases the risk of esophageal cancer.
        Esophageal cancer: Over time, persistent damage to the esophagus from GERD can
        increase the risk of esophageal cancer, particularly in those with Barrett’s esophagus.
        Dental erosion: Stomach acid that reaches the mouth can erode tooth enamel,
        leading to dental problems.

c.DIVERTICULOSIS AND DIVERTICULITIS

    Diverticulosis and diverticulitis are related conditions involving the formation of small
    pouches (called diverticula) in the walls of the colon (large intestine). While they are often
    mentioned together, they are distinct conditions, and understanding the differences,
    causes, symptoms, treatments, and prevention strategies is important for managing
    these disorders.
    Diverticulosis is a condition where small, bulging pouches (diverticula) form in the walls
    of the colon. These pouches develop when weak spots in the colon wall give way, and
    the inner layer of the intestine pushes through the outer layer. Diverticulosis most
    commonly occurs in the sigmoid colon, the lower part of the colon on the left side of the
    abdomen, but it can occur anywhere in the colon. Most people with diverticulosis do not
    experience symptoms, and it is often discovered incidentally during imaging tests (e.g.,
    colonoscopy or CT scan) for other issues.
    Diverticulitis occurs when one or more diverticula become inflamed or infected. This is
    typically due to food particles or bacteria becoming trapped in the diverticula, causing
    irritation, inflammation, and sometimes infection. Diverticulitis is a more serious condition
    than diverticulosis and can require medical treatment. In more severe cases, diverticulitis
    can cause complications such as abscesses, perforation (hole in the colon), bleeding, or
    peritonitis (infection in the abdominal cavity). These complications may require
    emergency treatment, including hospitalization or surgery.

d.INFLAMMATORY BOWEL DISEASE

    Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a group of chronic conditions that involve long-term
    inflammation of the digestive tract. The two most common types of IBD are Crohn's
    disease and ulcerative colitis. Both conditions can cause a wide range of symptoms and
    can significantly affect quality of life if not properly managed. IBD is different from irritable
    bowel syndrome (IBS) because it involves actual inflammation and damage to the
    digestive tract, whereas IBS is a functional disorder without inflammation. The exact
    cause of IBD is not fully understood, but several factors are believed to contribute to its
    development, including an autoimmune condition, genetics, certain environmental and
    dietary factors, infections, and smoking.

e.CROHN’S DISEASE

    Crohn's disease is a chronic inflammatory condition that affects the gastrointestinal (GI)
    tract, causing inflammation, pain, and other digestive issues.
    Crohn's disease can affect any part of the digestive tract, from the mouth to the anus, but
    it most commonly involves the ileum (the last part of the small intestine) and the colon
    (large intestine).
    In Crohn's disease, the body's immune system mistakenly attacks the GI tract, leading to
    inflammation that can damage the tissues.
    Unlike ulcerative colitis, which only affects the inner lining of the colon, Crohn's disease
    can cause inflammation throughout the entire thickness of the bowel wall and can occur
    anywhere along the GI tract. The inflammation is often patchy, meaning it can appear in
    sections of the digestive tract, with healthy tissue in between inflamed areas.

f. ULCERATIVE COLITIS

    Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) that primarily affects
    the colon (large intestine) and the rectum, causing long-term inflammation and sores
    (ulcers) in the lining of the colon.
    Ulcerative colitis is restricted to the colon and rectum, and the inflammation is usually
    continuous, meaning it tends to affect a large portion of the colon in a uniform pattern.
    In UC, the body's immune system incorrectly targets the lining of the colon, causing it to
    become inflamed. This inflammation leads to the development of small ulcers or open
    sores in the colon lining, which can cause bleeding, diarrhea, and other digestive
    problems.

g.PEPTIC ULCERS

    Peptic ulcers are open sores. There are three main types of peptic ulcers, based on
    where they occur:
    ‣ Gastric ulcers: These occur on the lining of the stomach.
    ‣ Duodenal ulcers: These occur in the duodenum (the first part of the small intestine
    after the stomach).
    ‣ Esophageal ulcers: These are less common and develop in the esophagus, often
    due to acid reflux or gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD).
    These sores are usually caused by H. pylori infection or the use of NSAIDs.
    Symptoms range from mild stomach pain to serious complications like bleeding or
    perforation. Treatment typically involves a combination of antibiotics (for H. pylori
    infection), acid-reducing medications, and lifestyle changes. With appropriate
    treatment, most ulcers heal completely.

3. BASAL METABOLIC RAT E

   When the body breaks down carbohydrates, fat, or protein for energy, the generation of
   heat is known as thermogenesis. There are three main categories of thermogenesis:
   - Energy used for basal metabolism
   - Energy for physical activity
   - Energy for food consumption
     Most of the energy we use every day supports our basal metabolism. Basal metabolism
     includes things like breathing, our heart beating, kidneys filtering wastes, and all the basic
     processes of life. Our basal metabolic rate (BMR ) is the rate at which the body expends
     energy for these life-sustaining activities.
     Another similar concept is the resting metabolic rate (RMR) which is always slightly

   there are many errors in estimating calorie intake and expenditure, so the overall
   contribution to energy balance is not of a great impact.

1. BODY COMPOSITION

   Body composition refers to the proportion of lean body mass (LBM) and body fat mass
   (BFM) within the body. LBM can be subdivided into muscle mass, body water, and bone
   mass.

2. MEASURING BODY MASS INDEX

   The most commonly used anthropometric assessments are height, weight, skinfold
   thickness, and waist circumference measurements.
   When measurements of height and weight are combined with measurements of waist and
   hip circumferences, a better assessment of long-term health risk may be obtained.
   Research suggests that fat in the abdominal area is associated with an increased risk for
   multiple chronic diseases including hypertension, diabetes, high cholesterol, and overall
   increased cardiovascular risk.
   Remember when we talked about body mass index (BMI)? You calculate BMI as a ratio of
   height to weight. You can use the equation BMI = kg/m^2 to calculate BMI.
   Weight classifications: healthy weight between a BMI of 18.5 and 24.9, underweight
   below 18.5, overweight above 25, and obese above 30.
   So, while it is important to know about BMI, and some people may use it as an indication
   of body composition and health, it is not an accurate measure of body composition.
   People of different ages, races, and genetic make-up have different BMIs.

3. METHODS TO MEASURE BODY FAT

   Skinfold thickness and waist circumference are used to measure body fat.
   When considering body fat, the location of the fat influences health just as much as the
   actual amount of fat. For example, much visceral fat stored around the major organs in
   the abdomen is referred to as central obesity or an apple shape. This is the most
   dangerous storage form of visceral fat and contributes to multiple cancers, diabetes, and
   heart disease.
   A person’s waist circumference is a good indicator of central obesity and its associated
   health risks. Women with a waist circumference >35 inches and men with a waist
   circumference >40 inches have higher risk of health problems.
   Another way to measure body composition includes underwater weighing. Underwater
   weighing, or hydrostatic weighing, is based on Archimedes principle, that when a body is
   submerged in water, there's a buoyant counterforce equal to the weight of the water
   that's displaced. A person with lean muscle and bone will be denser because those
   tissues are denser than water, and they will weigh more in water.
   Another way to measure body composition is bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA). This
   method uses a low level electrical current that's sent through the body. The resistance to
   that current by the tissues is measured and because lean tissue has more water than fat,
   it conducts the signal faster than fat. The error rate for BIA is about 3% and is higher than
   1.5% error for underwater weighing. However, both of these are much more accurate
   than waist circumference, BMI, and skinfold measures.

4. BODY WEIGHT AND MORTALITY

   While body weight and body fat correlate with chronic disease risk and life expectancy,
   not all overweight and underweight people will get a chronic disease just as those who
   are at healthy weights don’t always avoid chronic disease.
   People who are extremely underweight or extremely obese carry higher risks of early
   deaths than those whose weights fall within the healthy or even the slightly overweight
   range, and those with extremely high body weights are more likely than those with normal
   weight to have chronic diseases.

   Exercise is a planned, structured, and repetitive movement pattern intended to improve
   fitness.
   Health benefits of reducing your sedentary activity include better cardiovascular fitness
   and reduced risk of chronic disease. Unfortunately, the majority (80%) of adults in the

   Benefits of physically active lifestyles include: better sleep, reduced stress, enhanced
   bone density, better immunity, lower risk of cardiovascular disease and diabetes, and
   lower levels of anxiety and depression.

2. COMPONENTS OF HEALTH-RELATED FITNESS

   In order to carry out activities of daily living, a minimal level of fitness is required. There
   are five main areas where fitness is needed: cardiorespiratory endurance, muscular
   strength and endurance, flexibility, and body composition. These five areas are called the
   components of health-related fitness.
   - Cardiorespiratory endurance is the ability to carry out prolonged, large muscle,
     dynamic movements at a moderate to high level of intensity. This relates to your
     heart’s ability to pump blood and your lungs’ ability to take in oxygen.
   - Muscular strength is the ability of the muscles to exert force over a single or maximal
     effort. Examples of how you can increase your muscular strength are consistently
     lifting weights, using resistance bands, or doing push-ups.
   - Muscular endurance is the ability to exert a force over a period of time or repetitions
     and might include aerobic dance or circuit training. Also notice that there are some
     activities that overlap categories. For example, swimming can be a cardiorespiratory
     and muscular endurance exercise.
   - Flexibility is the ability to move your joints through a full range of motion.

3. FITT

   The human body generally adapts well when exposed to stress in the context of exercise.
   This stress is defined as an exertion above the normal, everyday functioning.

   In exercise, the amount of stress placed on the body can be controlled by four variables:
   Frequency, Intensity, Time (duration), and Type, known as FITT.

4. FUEL FOR PHYSICAL ACTIVITY

   When we are active, our muscles use the oxygen from the blood, and it is used to make
   ATP. When we are physically active, our muscles need more energy and more oxygen. To
   provide more oxygen, our heart rate and breathing rate increase.
   When we first begin being active, our muscles respond first and use the ATP that is
   stored in them. This stored ATP only lasts a few seconds before it is used. Creatine
   phosphate is another stored source of energy that can be converted to adenosine
   diphosphate (ADP) and then to ATP. It is also used quickly. After a few minutes of
   exercise, your heart rate and breathing should have increased to supply more oxygen to
   your muscles, so you do not have to rely on anaerobic metabolism. The primary source to
   make ATP is carbohydrate, but fats and protein can also be used.
   Glucose, a type of sugar you get from foods you eat, and your body uses for energy, can
   come from our blood glucose or muscle glycogen, and it is the primary source of energy.
   Fatty acids (from dietary fat) are stored in muscles as triglycerides, and when you are
   exercising at a low or moderate rate, the fatty acids can become the major source for
   energy. Protein is not a good source of energy even though a few amino acids can be
   used while you are active.
   The intensity of exercise determines what energy sources are used for ATP production.
   For example, sprinting is a short burst of intense activity, so it is anaerobic (does not
   need oxygen) and glucose is the preferred fuel source. In contrast, walking for an hour is
   a low intensity activity that is aerobic and will most likely use fat as the fuel source. Low-
   intensity activity uses aerobic metabolism because it is more efficient and fatty acids can
   be mobilized to provide the energy source.

2. DIETARY SUPPLEMENTS

   Dietary supplements are regulated by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) but, do
   not have to go through review by the FDA for safety or effectiveness or be approved
   before they can be marketed.
   DSHEA establishes specific labeling requirements, provides a regulatory framework, and
   authorizes the FDA to establish current regulations for dietary supplements. However, the
   DSHEA redefines "dietary supplements" and "dietary ingredients" and classifies them as
   food - meaning they are no longer subject the FDAs drug regulations (which assured the
   drug was safe and truthfully labelled).

3. THE REGULATION OF DIETARY SUPPLEMENTS

   Dietary supplements don't have to go through review by the FDA for safety or
   effectiveness or be approved before they can be marketed. However, manufacturers
   must provide pre-market notice and evidence of safety for any supplements they plan to
   sell that contain certain dietary ingredients that were not in the market before the DSHEA.
   2000 - FDA published a rule on dietary supplements that defined the type of labeling
   claim that can be made on product labels regarding the effect of supplements on the
   structure or function of the body.
   2003 - the FDA published a rule for current Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP). These
   are requirements for dietary supplements to prevent mistakes with ingredients, the
   presence of contaminants, mislabeling, and improper packaging.
   FDA conducts over random inspections of dietary supplement manufacturers. The most
   common GMP violations are the failure to test products for identity, purity, and potency.
   2004 - the FDA banned dietary supplements containing ephedra, a naturally occurring
   substance found in plants, because of concern over their cardiovascular effects. So far,
   the FDA has only banned two supplements due to issues with safety: Ephedra sinica and
   dimethylamylamine.
   Under DSHEA, manufacturers must list the ingredients in each supplement in a
   standardized format. This is the supplement facts label. They are also only permitted to
   make structure-function claims about dietary supplements. Ex: An acceptable structure-
   function claim is “Supports strong bones.” Manufacturers of dietary supplements are not,
   however, allowed to make claims such as “Prevents osteoporosis.”
   The supplement facts label must also include the following structure-function disclaimer:
   “This statement has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. This
   product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease”.
   Manufacturers are currently only required to report information about structure-function
   claims and safety information when a product contains a new dietary ingredient (NDI). A
   dietary ingredient is considered an NDI if it was not used prior to 1994. The FDA still has
   the authority to remove supplements from the shelves after it enters the market, but it can
   only do so if it has proven that the product is unsafe or that the labeling is false or
   misleading. With so many dietary supplements available, this can be a difficult task.

a.REGULATING SUPPLEMENTS ONLINE

    It is even more difficult for the FDA to regulate supplements that are sold online because
    it is one of the fastest growing markets for retail. On the internet, consumers can easily
    locate dietary supplements that contain banned ingredients.

b.THE FTC’S ROLE IN REGULATING SUPPLEMENTS

    While the FDA is responsible for recalling unsafe and mislabeled supplements, it is the
    Federal Trade Commission’s (FTC) responsibility to prevent false advertisement of
    products on broadcasts/media/internet. Just as the FDA cannot perform its role prior to
    distribution, the FTC also lacks the authority to regulate the advertising of dietary
    supplements on these platforms before they are available for purchase by consumers.

4. EDUCATING YOURSELF ON SUPPLEMENTS

   dietary supplements are no longer allowed to place product information near products.
   Consumers are therefore responsible for educating themselves on any supplement they
   want to use before consuming it. For credible information and FDA warnings about
   dietary supplements, consumers can go to the Office of Dietary Supplements’ (ODS)
   website.Safety of Dietary Supplements
   The safety of dietary supplements is very important, but another consideration is the
   nutrient and drug interactions that may occur. Some supplements, such as St. John's
   wort and goldenseal, are known to cause drug interactions.

2. DRAWBACKS TO HERBAL SUPPLEMENTS

   While there are many potential benefits of using herbal supplements, not many of them
   have been strongly supported by research.

   - Manufacturers of the supplements are responsible for ensuring the efficacy and

3. POTENTIAL RISKS OF USING HERBAL SUPPLEMENTS

   overuse, herbal toxicity, and drug nutrient interactions (DNIs ). Some herbal
   supplements that may be beneficial in small doses can be fatal in large doses.
   Ex: Licorice root, which can be used to treat gastric ulcers, can cause high blood
   pressure, hypokalemia, and heart failure when consumed in large amounts.

   susceptible to toxic injury. It can be difficult to determine the exact cause of toxic injury

   a toxic herb which can cause fatal hepatitis.

   cancel out the effects of medications. Not many herbs commonly used in the United
   States pose a great risk for DNI. Herbs that have a high risk for DNI include St. John’s
   wort, goldenseal, and black pepper in supplemental form. The risk of DNI increases
   when herbs are combined or used in concentrated forms. Because medical professionals
   are often unaware of any herbal supplements their clients are taking, it can be difficult to
   take preventative measures for DNI. Always tell your doctor about supplements.

2. 2. TYPES OF WEIGHT LOSS DRUGS

   Most prescription weight-loss drugs work by decreasing appetite and/or increasing
   feelings of fullness. The only drug that does not work like this is orlistat, and it works by
   interfering with the absorption of fat.

a.APPETITE SUPPRESSANTS

    Medications that primarily work by reducing appetite.
    Phentermine (Adipex-P, Lomaira) - oldest and most widely used weight loss drug,
    stimulates the release of norepinephrine in the brain, suppressing appetite. Often
    prescribed short-term as part of a comprehensive weight loss program, including lifestyle
    changes and behavior modification, it may also be prescribed long-term. Common side
    effects include increased blood pressure, heart palpitations, dry mouth, insomnia, and
    nervousness. Due to cardiovascular related side effects, phentermine is contraindicated
    in individuals with a history of cardiovascular disease, hyperthyroidism, or drug abuse.
    Diethylpropion (Tenuate) - Diethylpropion is similar to phentermine because it stimulates
    the release of norepinephrine, decreasing appetite, often prescribed for short-term use.
    Similarly, diethylpropion has significant side effects related to cardiovascular health, so
    people with a history of heart disease or drug abuse should not use this.

b.FAT ABSORPTION INHIBITORS

    Prescription medications for weight loss that work to reduce the absorption of fat from
    the digestive tract are known as fat absorption inhibitors.
    Orlistat - one of the only medications of this type approved for use in the United States
    and is branded as Alli or Xenical. Orlistat inhibits pancreatic lipase, an enzyme necessary
    for the breakdown of dietary fats in the intestines. As a result, a portion of dietary fat is
    not absorbed and is excreted in the stool. Unlike appetite suppressants, orlistat may be
    used as a long-term treatment option for weight loss. An over-the-counter medication is
    available in 60 mg capsules that patients are instructed to take with each meal containing
    fat. The main side effects of orlistat are diarrhea, abdominal discomfort, bloating, and oily
    stools because dietary fat is not being absorbed. Following a low-fat diet can reduce the
    side effects. Additionally, because orlistat inhibits the absorption of dietary fat, some fat-
    soluble vitamins and certain medications may not be absorbed properly.

c.COMBINATION MEDICATIONS

    Some medications combine multiple mechanisms to assist with weight loss.
    Phentermine/Topiramate (Qsymia) - This combination drug pairs phentermine (appetite
    suppressant) with topiramate, a medication used to treat epilepsy and migraines (to
    promote a feeling of fullness). In the United States, Qsymia is approved for long-term
    weight management. Side effects of this drug include those of phentermine, but also
    include some from the topiramate. Dry mouth, insomnia, constipation, dizziness, and an
    increased risk of heart rate acceleration, as well as mood changes and memory problems
    may result from taking this medication. Topiramate can cause birth defects, so people
    who are pregnant or planning on becoming pregnant should not take this drug.
    Naltrexone/Bupropion (Contrave ) - Contrave is a brand-name weight-loss drug that
    combines naltrexone, which is an opioid antagonist, and bupropion, which is an atypical
    antidepressant. Together, these drugs reduce food cravings and improve mood, which
    may result in better appetite control. Contrave is approved in the United States for long-
    term weight management even though it has several side effects. Nausea, constipation,
    headache, dizziness, and insomnia, as well as increased blood pressure and risk for
    seizures. Because of these side effects, this drug should not be used by individuals with
    a history of seizures, substance abuse, or eating disorders.

d.GLUCAGON-LIKE PEPTIDE-1 (GLP-1) AGONISTS

    A more recent class of drugs originally used to treat type 2 diabetes. GLP-1 Agonists
    promote weight loss by increasing feelings of fullness and slowing gastric emptying.
    Liraglutide (Saxenda) - a GLP-1 receptor agonist that increases insulin secretion in
    response to meals, decreases glucagon secretion, and promotes satiety, leading to
    reduced food intake. This daily injectable drug is approved in the United States for weight

management in adults who have obesity or overweight and at least one weight-related
condition. There are side effects to taking liraglutide, which include nausea, diarrhea,
constipation, headache, and even pancreatitis or gallbladder disease.

    Semaglutide (Wegovy, Ozempic ) - a GLP-1 receptor agonist similar to liraglutide but is
    longer-acting. Wegovy, the brand name for semaglutide, is approved in the United States
    for weight management in patients with obesity or overweight with a weight-related
    health condition. It helps regulate blood sugar levels and promotes weight loss by
    enhancing feelings of fullness and reducing hunger. Ozempic is the brand name approved
    for the treatment of type 2 diabetes. Semaglutide is administered as an injection and has
    several reported side effects—including nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation, and
    abdominal pain—in addition to pancreatitis, gallbladder disease, and kidney issues.
    Tirzepatide (Zepbound, Mounjaro) - Tirzepatide, previously approved as Mounjaro for
    type 2 diabetes, is now FDA-approved as Zepbound for obesity in adults with a BMI

        =30. It’s a new class of drug that combines GLP-1 and GIP receptor agonists, similar to
        semaglutide, reducing appetite and intended for weight loss when combined with diet
        and exercise. Administered as an injection, common side effects include nausea,
        vomiting, diarrhea, decreased appetite, constipation, and abdominal pain.

    A medical device, known as Plenity or Hydrogel, was approved by the FDA in 2019 for
    the treatment of obesity and overweight in people with a BMI of 24–40. This medication is
    a capsule that releases a super-absorbent hydrogel into the stomach, which increases
    satiety and helps you to eat less. The gel can occupy up to at least one-quarter of your
    stomach before passing to the large intestine and being excreted in stool. Patients with
    GERD, ulcers, or other gastrointestinal issues should use this device with caution.
    In the United States, generic alternatives are generally more affordable, and currently, few
    options are old enough to have generic options. Insurance coverage and personal
    preferences around injectables or pills will determine which options are best for a patient.

a.GASTRIC SLEEVE

    With the gastric sleeve, a surgeon removes most of the stomach, leaving only a banana-
    shaped section that is closed with staples. The surgery reduces the amount of food that
    can fit in the stomach. Taking out part of the stomach affects hormones and bacteria in
    the gastrointestinal tract that impact appetite and metabolism. This type of surgery
    cannot be reversed because some of the stomach is permanently removed.

b.GASTRIC BYPASS

    Gastric bypass surgery, also called Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, is done in three steps.
        The surgeon staples the stomach, creating a small pouch in the upper section. The
        staples make the stomach much smaller, so you eat less because you feel full sooner.
        The surgeon divides the small intestine into two parts and attaches the lower part
        directly to the small stomach pouch. Food will bypass most of the stomach and the
        upper part of the small intestine, so your body absorbs fewer calories.
        The surgeon then reconnects the upper part of the small intestine to a new location
        farther down on the lower part of the small intestine. This allows digestive juices in
        the stomach to flow from the bypassed part of the small intestine to the lower part of
        the small intestine, so that food can be fully digested.
    The bypass changes hormones, bacteria, and other substances in the gastrointestinal
    tract that may affect appetite and metabolism. Gastric bypass is difficult to reverse,
    although a surgeon may do it if medically necessary.

c.ADJUSTABLE GASTRIC BAND

    The surgeon places a ring with an inflatable band around the top of the stomach to create
    a small pouch. Like gastric sleeve and bypass surgery, the band makes you feel full after
    eating small amounts of food. The inner band has a saline-filled balloon. The surgeon
    adjusts the band’s size by injecting or removing saline through a small device under the

skin. After surgery, you’ll need follow-up visits to adjust the band opening. If the band
causes problems or isn’t helping, the surgeon may remove it.

d.BILIOPANCREATIC DIVERSION WITH DUODENAL SWITCH

    This complicated and risky surgery involves two separate procedures. The first procedure
    is similar to gastric sleeve surgery. A second surgical procedure divides the small
    intestine into two tracts. Food moves through one tract, bypassing most of the small
    intestine. This reduces the number of calories and amount of nutrients absorbed.
    Digestive juices flow from the stomach through the other intestinal tract and mix with
    food as it enters the colon.

1. ELIMINATION DIETS

   Elimination diet - a food / food group is removed for a period of time to help determine if
   specific foods contribute to symptoms. Changes a single dietary variable at a time.
   The process of developing and following an elimination diet can take a lot of time and
   effort from the healthcare practitioner, registered dietitian, and the patient. However,
   successfully discovering food allergies or intolerances can potentially reduce the severity
   of some diseases and may help lessen uncomfortable symptoms.
   With food allergies or reactions, blood serum tests are available to help diagnose the
   issue. However, these tests have low specificity and sensitivity, so using a food
   elimination diet may be the gold standard for discovering allergies or intolerances. Many
   of the blood serum allergy tests are offered by private laboratories and focus on IgG
   levels and not all food allergies are of this type of immune response.

2. FIGURING OUT WHICH FOOD(S) TO ELIMINATE

   Before planning any part of an elimination diet, you should work with a registered dietitian
   and your healthcare provider to make sure that an elimination diet is clinically appropriate
   and something that is not dangerous to your health situation.
   You will most likely be asked to keep a food record to see if there are any nutrient
   deficiencies or other issues before you start. Your healthcare team should be involved
   since there are potential risks.

3. THE AVOIDANCE PHASE AND ELIMINATING FOODS

   Step two is eliminating foods. The intensity of the diet depends primarily on how many
   foods are eliminated at a time. There are three levels that include low, medium, and high
   complexity.
   Low complexity diet: The simple or low complexity diet eliminates milk, eggs, and
   wheat. This level is better for people at risk of undernutrition or who might have difficulty
   sticking with the more complex elimination diet. This diet plan will not help to determine
   intolerance to many food groups, just the general three listed.
   Moderate complexity diet : A stricter diet that eliminates several food groups including
   dairy, wheat, eggs, all legumes, nuts, some specific fruits and vegetables, artificial
   sweeteners, all animal fats, many vegetables fats, chocolate, coffee, tea, soft drinks, and
   alcohol. This diet has high rates of success in determining many kinds of food
   intolerances without significant nutritional risks. However, a lot of planning, recording
   food intake, new grocery purchases, recipe trials, and time must be invested.
   High complexity diet: This diet is also known as the few-foods diet. It eliminates all but a
   few foods. This might be used if you have a long list of potential food intolerances and
   want to start fresh with reintroducing foods. This diet has the highest chance for
   symptom improvement but requires supervision from healthcare professionals (registered
   dietitians), may be expensive, and includes significant time for the reintroduction process.

4. THE CHALLENGING PHASE: REINTRODUCING THE ELIMINATED FOODS

   In step three of the elimination diet, we follow the “Rule of 3s”. There are 3 weeks of
   elimination, then a food challenge during all 3 meals of 1 day, then you wait 3 days before
   another rechallenge.
   Eliminating a food is not enough to determine if it might be a problem, so reintroducing
   the food will provide another way to see if there is an association between the foods you
   eat and any symptoms you may experience. Because it can take several days for
   symptoms to reappear, a 3-day waiting period after reintroduction is needed.

5. THE MAINTENANCE PHASE

   Step four, the final step, is the maintenance phase. After the elimination diet has been
   completed, you can work with your registered dietitian to make a new diet that removes
   specific foods or food groups long term.
   If you are avoiding specific food groups that are associated with specific vitamins or
   nutrients (like the calcium in dairy), other foods that are tolerated might need to be eaten
   in higher amounts to make up for these deficits (e.g., increasing leafy green intake if you
   are avoiding dairy foods). Foods that cause an IgE-mediated food allergy should NOT be
   reintroduced.
   Keep in mind that food allergies, food sensitivity, and food intolerances are different. A
   food allergy happens when your body overreacts to the proteins in specific foods. Allergic
   reactions can include skin irritation, hives, rashes, itchiness, swelling and anaphylaxis.
   Food intolerances are when your body can’t properly break down a certain food or
   ingredient, and you might experience GI issues like diarrhea, gas, and bloating. Food
   sensitivities can be due to an imbalance of the bacteria in your gastrointestinal tract.

b.MECHANICAL SOFT DIET

    The mechanical soft diet consists of foods soft in texture, moderately low in fiber, and
    processed by chopping, grinding or pureeing to be easier to chew. This diet is
    appropriate for people who have difficulty chewing or swallowing and have been
    evaluated by a registered dietitian and speech language pathologist. Most milk products,
    tender meats, mashed potatoes, tender vegetables, and fruits and their juices are
    included in the diet.

c.CLEAR LIQUID DIET

    This is a short-term diet that provides clear liquids to supply fluid and calories without
    residue. It is often used with acute illness, before and after surgery, or other procedures.
    The clear liquid diet includes coffee, tea, clear juices, gelatin and clear broth.

d.FULL LIQUID DIET

    As a transition between clear liquid and a soft or regular diet, this plan provides easily
    tolerated foods. Patients in the hospital who are transitioning from a clear liquid diet to
    another diet would need this temporarily. The diet includes milk, strained and creamed
    soups, grits, creamed cereal, and fruit and vegetable juices.

e.LOW RESIDUE/LOW FIBER DIET

    This type of diet tries to limit fiber and is designed to minimize the frequency and volume
    of residue in the intestinal tract. This diet is appropriate for patients who are experiencing
    gastrointestinal difficulties or those who have certain gastrointestinal diseases.

f. SALT-RESTRICTED (LOW SODIUM) DIET

    Sodium controlled diets are usually prescribed for patients with hypertension and for
    those with excess fluid accumulation (edema) or heart disease.

g.FAT-RESTRICTED DIET

    This diet is often prescribed for patients with gastrointestinal disorders or obesity.

h.CHOLESTEROL-RESTRICTED DIET

    Lowering blood cholesterol can reduce your risk of heart disease so this diet is common
    for people who have a history of high cholesterol, high triglycerides, or heart disease.
    Certain oats, beans, and fruits are effective at lowering cholesterol levels in the body, so a
    cholesterol-restricted diet limits the intake of meats, poultry, fried foods, egg yolks, and
    whole milk products.

i. CONSISTENT CARBOHYDRATE (DIABETIC DIET)

    A diabetic diet is developed depending on the type and intensity of the individual
    person’s diabetes, their personal history, and individual nutrient needs. Meals are similar
    to those found on a regular menu, but carbohydrate servings are carefully controlled and
    small snacks may be included in the meal plan.

j. RENAL DIET

    A renal diet is carefully planned with special consideration of nutrients that are important
    to your kidneys. Patients who are on dialysis will have a different diet plan than those who
    are not on dialysis. Transplant patients may also have different needs. If dialysis
    treatments are not used, the intake of foods such as potatoes, tomatoes, oranges, and
    bananas may be restricted.

k.GLUTEN-FREE DIET

    A gluten-free diet is an eating plan that excludes foods containing gluten. Gluten is a
    protein found in wheat, barley, rye, and triticale (a cross between wheat and rye). A
    gluten-free diet is essential for people managing signs and symptoms of celiac disease
    and other medical conditions associated with gluten.

VOCAB
Therapeutic Diet: A meal plan that controls the intake of certain foods or nutrients.
